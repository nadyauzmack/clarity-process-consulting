What a Three-Hour Review Taught Me About Process Improvement

Firsthand operational process improvement perspective on why complex reviews take too long, how rework adds up, and lessons from reducing review times.

From the experience of Nadya Uzmack · Founder, Clarity Process Consulting

In my corporate operations work, I've seen how seemingly small inefficiencies can become normal when a team handles complex, high-volume cases every day. Among the improvements I've supported, review time moved from approximately three hours to one and a half hours. What matters to me is not just the number—it is understanding how workflows become easier to execute correctly.

Why a long review isn't always evidence of a difficult case

Some reviews genuinely require careful analysis. Others become longer because information is dispersed across systems, employees must confirm the same requirement more than once, or instructions don't clearly tell them what counts as complete. From inside operations, I learned not to judge the person doing the work before examining the process around them.

When a reviewer has to repeatedly search for evidence, interrupt someone for clarification, or reopen a case after a missing requirement is found late, the entire workflow absorbs that friction. It can look like an individual productivity issue even when the underlying design makes repetition inevitable.

The question I ask before proposing a solution

I begin with: Which steps actually change the quality of this decision? That doesn't mean every repeated check is unnecessary. In regulated processes some verification is there for a reason. The purpose of an assessment is to distinguish a truly independent control from checking something that's already established.

Then I look at how the procedure explains the decision. Does it name the required inputs? Can a reviewer tell which conditions call for an exception? Does the system record what was already verified? Can a new employee follow the workflow without collecting the same answer from three colleagues?

Make the real process visible

I have consulted on case reviews, operational requirements, job aids, cross-team exceptions and workflow improvements. The useful information is often in the gap between the formal SOP and what people do when the SOP doesn't answer the question.

For remote consulting, that means asking someone to screen-share the workflow using appropriate sample information. I want to see where they pause, switch screens, look for guidance and hand work to another team. A process map based only on a manager's description usually misses the expensive parts.

What the outcome means—and what it doesn't

I supported operational improvement work associated with review time changing from about three hours to roughly 1.5 hours. I also reported an improvement in average error rates from around 10% to 4% across my operational experience. These are approximate team-environment outcomes, not a claim that one action or one person exclusively produced every result.

I use those examples to show the kind of work I understand: balancing accuracy, process clarity, practical changes, automation readiness and employee enablement. I don't promise that a new client will see the same percentages.

What I would recommend to a company with the same problem

Start with a bounded review workflow. Define the expected outcome, study representative tasks, separate touch time from total cycle time, identify repeated steps and missing decision criteria, and document where employees need better guidance. Only then decide what to remove, standardize or automate.
