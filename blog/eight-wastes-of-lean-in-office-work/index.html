8 Wastes of Lean in Office Work: A Corporate Workflow Guide

Find eight types of Lean waste in administrative processes, approvals, insurance workflows, and service operations.

Educational guide: Illustrative scenarios are examples, not claims about specific client projects.

Why office waste is harder to see

Employees can spend an entire day reconciling trackers, chasing approvals, and searching for documents while the customer receives no faster or better outcome. Lean distinguishes activity from value and encourages redesigning systems rather than simply telling people to move faster.

The eight wastes are commonly summarized as defects, overproduction, waiting, non-utilized talent, transportation, inventory, motion, and extra-processing.

Defects and extra-processing

Defects: a request reaches an approver without required information, returns for correction, and must be resubmitted. Extra-processing: three employees verify the same unchanged field without independent reason, or a team types data already captured in another system.

Address incomplete inputs, inconsistent criteria, and repeat work through standardization and targeted validation. Keep genuinely necessary independent quality checks.

Waiting and inventory

Waiting: cases sit unassigned, or an approval waits because escalation rules are unclear. Inventory: unfinished requests accumulate in inboxes, queues, and spreadsheets.

Measure how long work is actively handled versus how long it sits idle. This can reveal that queue design matters more than the speed of an individual task.

Motion and transportation

Motion: searching through several folders for the correct version of an SOP. Transportation: a request bouncing among departments without becoming more complete.

Create one source of truth, visible case ownership, and an explicit handoff trigger. Remove transfers that have no clear purpose.

Overproduction and unused talent

Overproduction: generating reports nobody uses, duplicating status emails, or collecting data unrelated to a decision. Non-utilized talent: experienced staff understand why a process fails, but are never included in its redesign.

Interview employees who perform the work, not only managers. Their workarounds can expose root causes missing from official process maps.

Start small, then measure

Choose one process with measurable friction. Map an actual request from intake through completion, label the wastes you observe, and pilot targeted changes.

Combine waste identification with DMAIC and value stream mapping rather than treating the eight wastes as a one-time checklist.
