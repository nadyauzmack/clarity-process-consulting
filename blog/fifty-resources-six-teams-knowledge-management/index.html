50+ Learning Resources and Six Teams: My Lessons in Knowledge Management

Real corporate knowledge management perspective from developing 50+ process and training resources, a SharePoint reference system, and training across six teams.

From the experience of Nadya Uzmack · Founder, Clarity Process Consulting

One of the parts of corporate operations I enjoy most is taking complicated information and making it usable. I've created more than 50 learning and process resources across operational initiatives, helped build a SharePoint knowledge system, and delivered training across six teams. That work taught me that documentation is an operational design problem—not just a writing task.

Why a document can exist and still not help anyone

I've consulted with teams that need answers quickly while navigating detailed requirements. A procedure buried in an old folder or written for a different role can be effectively invisible. The employee asks a colleague, the colleague forwards another reference, and the same question returns next week.

A searchable, current knowledge base reduces that dependency. But the goal isn't simply to upload every existing document into SharePoint. It's to build a structure that follows the work employees actually perform.

Build guidance around decisions and tasks

For processes involving different roles and specialties, I favor direct task descriptions, clear triggers, step-by-step procedures, common exceptions, defined owners, and links to the current forms or systems. A salesperson, case reviewer and training lead may need different views of the same requirement.

In my prior work, I developed job aids, role-based guidance, procedural materials and other learning assets as workflows, launches and requirements evolved. That role-specific framing made it possible to train several teams without pretending everyone needed the exact same level of detail.

Training six teams reinforced the importance of listening

Training is a valuable test of a process. When several employees ask the same question, the answer may belong in an SOP. When departments interpret a requirement differently, the handoff or decision rule may need to be clarified. A good training program gathers that feedback instead of just presenting slides.

Cross-functional collaboration was part of my experience; no document survives contact with real work unchanged. An owner should update guidance when the process evolves and retire older versions.

What the 50+ figure does—and doesn't—mean

The scale of my experience includes more than 50 resources across corporate improvement and enablement work, not 50 new client deliverables produced by this consultancy. The count shows repeated practice translating complex operational information into useful materials.

I don't assign an invented dollar value or claim an unsupported productivity percentage to that work. Its professional significance lies in accessible instructions, clearer knowledge transfer and more sustainable team workflows.

How I'd apply this experience to a new client's SOPs

First, I review the current documents and ask employees to show which ones they actually use. Then I map the process, clarify decisions and ownership, draft the future-state instructions and test them with representatives of the team. The output needs to remain easy to maintain.
