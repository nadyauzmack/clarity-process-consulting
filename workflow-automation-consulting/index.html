Automation readiness · Workflow design

Automate the right steps—not the confusion.

The goal is not to automate everything. The goal is to reduce unnecessary work while keeping decisions clear, exceptions manageable, and controls effective.

Why some automations disappoint

A manual reminder may look like a good automation candidate. But if employees lack a clear owner for the next step, reminders merely move the bottleneck. Likewise, automating a confusing intake form can generate bad data more quickly.

Start with current-state mapping: what triggers the task, where inputs come from, which decisions follow explicit rules and where expert judgment is still necessary.

My experience with automation adoption

In enterprise operations I supported the adoption of 12 automation solutions. Areas of work included correspondence, validation and operations-related workflows, alongside requirements clarification, testing feedback, job aids and user enablement.

My documented role was process and adoption support; that is different from claiming I personally programmed every automation. I focus on whether the business process is ready, the rules make sense and employees can follow the future state.

What an automation assessment can deliver

A remote process audit can identify candidates for standardization and automation, distinguish quick wins from risky changes, and recommend how to proceed. A subsequent redesign engagement can map the future-state workflow and create SOPs for the revised steps.

Actual software configuration, system cleaning and data migration require an agreed scope, suitable permissions, and sometimes specialist support. Do not share credentials or sensitive records through the public contact form.

Where automation readiness applies

SaaS customer onboarding, repeat correspondence, customer support routing, HR administrative steps, e-commerce returns, financial back-office processing, procurement coordination and similar digital workflows can all benefit from a process-first approach.
